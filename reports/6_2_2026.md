## 1. Project Overview and Research Questions

The central hypothesis is that **Chow-Liu (CL) trees** — maximum spanning trees of pairwise mutual information (MI) graphs — are a superior model of brain functional connectivity compared to standard Pearson-correlation FC, with three claimed advantages:

1. **Sparsity / ground-truth recovery.** Real structural connectivity is sparse and tree-like in many circuits. FC produces a dense N×N matrix that conflates direct dependencies with indirect (transitive) paths; the CL tree enforces a parsimonious spanning tree that, under the tree-distribution assumption (Chow & Liu 1968), maximally preserves the joint information with the fewest edges.

2. **Nonlinearity sensitivity.** FC (Pearson r) is blind to phase coupling, quadrature coupling, and other nonlinear dependencies. MI is sensitive to all statistical dependencies; CL trees built on MI therefore detect connectivity that FC misses.

3. **Inter-individual conservation.** A hypothesis (originating from a discussion with supervisor Milo De Mello, documented in `code/simulations/notes/noise.md`) proposes that CL trees extract a more stable, conserved backbone of brain organization across individuals, while FC's density captures noise-driven variability that inflates apparent individual differences.

The project addresses these via two parallel tracks:

| Track | Driver | Question |
|---|---|---|
| **Empirical** | MSC, ABIDE, LISTEN datasets | What do CL trees reveal about real resting-state and task connectivity? |
| **Simulation** | Generic2dOscillator neural-mass model | Under what conditions does CL outperform FC, and why? |

The most mature empirical focus is the **motor system**, specifically a Group-Constrained Subject-Specific (GCSS) parcellation of motor cortex built from the MSC motor task, with full analyses of 11 motor ROIs across all 10 MSC subjects.

### 2.3 Mutual Information Computation

Pairwise MI is computed in PyTorch (GPU-accelerated) using the histogram estimator:
1. Discretize each ROI time series to K=100 bins via `torch.bucketize`.
2. Encode joint bins as K·a + b; compute joint probability via `torch.bincount`.
3. Marginal probability from row/column sums; product of marginals via `torch.outer`.
4. MI = Σ p(a,b) · log[p(a,b) / p(a)p(b)], with zero-count clamping at 1e-10.

**Limitation:** The histogram estimator converges to the true MI at large T but is biased upward at small T (positive bias ~K²/T for uniform distributions; see Paninski 2003). With K=100 bins and T~8000 TRs, this is negligible for the all-sessions concatenation but non-trivial for single sessions (~800 TRs).

### 2.4 Chow-Liu Tree Construction

```python
nx.maximum_spanning_tree(G, algorithm='kruskal', weight='weight')
```

where `G` is a fully-connected weighted `nx.Graph` with MI as edge weights. This produces the unique spanning tree that maximizes total MI weight. Under the tree-distribution assumption (Chow & Liu 1968), this is the information-maximally consistent dependency tree — i.e., the tree-structured distribution that minimizes KL divergence to the empirical joint distribution.

**Important distinction:** The CL tree is the best tree-structured approximation to the joint distribution. It is *not* guaranteed to recover structural connectivity, but it encodes the direct dependencies in the most information-preserving sparse form.

### 2.5 Simulation Engine

Neural-mass model: **Generic2dOscillator** (TVB equivalent):
```
dV = d·τ·(−f·V³ + e·V² + g·V + α·W + γ·coupling)
dW = (d/τ)·(c·V² + b·V − β·W + a)
```

Integrated with **Heun stochastic** (predict-correct) using PyTorch, CUDA-accelerated. Per-node heterogeneous parameters drawn from TVB defaults with Gaussian variability (b_σ=0.5, τ_σ=0.15). Five coupling types implemented: LINEAR, QUADRATURE, RECTIFIED, SQUARED, PAC.

Ground-truth connectivity types: `tree` (Prüfer random tree), `hierarchical` (BFS branching), `erdos_renyi` (density 0.1–0.9), `dense`.

## 3. Simulation Results

### 3.1 CL vs. FC Recovery on Known Ground Truth

The sweep framework (`sweep_sim.py`) evaluates seven connectivity-reconstruction methods against four metrics (F1, sensitivity, specificity, Pearson) across five network sizes (N = 5, 10, 15, 20, 25) and 10 trials each:

| Method | Description |
|---|---|
| `fc_significance` | Pearson r, FDR-corrected (Fisher-z) |
| `fc_density` | Pearson r, top-N edges |
| `fc_matched` | Pearson r, density-matched to GT |
| `mi_raw` | Raw MI (all pairs) |
| `mi_density` | Top-N MI edges |
| `mi_matched` | MI, density-matched |
| `cl_adjacency` | CL tree (N−1 edges) |

**Key finding:** CL trees significantly outperform FC on **sparse, tree-structured ground truths** (F1 improvement documented in `sweep_results.npz`). On dense ground truths, CL performance degrades relative to FC because forcing N−1 edges onto a densely connected graph necessarily misses most GT edges.

This is the fundamental trade-off: CL is the correct model when the GT is sparse (tree-like circuits); it is inappropriate when GT is dense (hub-and-spoke, small-world networks). Real brain functional connectivity is believed to be sparse at the level of direct anatomical connections, making CL the better approximation if the goal is to recover structural circuit topology.

### 3.3 Noise Robustness (`noise_sim.py`)

Tested on a fixed 15-node random tree, 5 individuals differing only in noise amplitude D ∈ {1e-5, 5e-5, 2e-4, 8e-4, 3e-3}. Metrics: Jaccard similarity of inferred edge sets to GT, and pairwise Jaccard across individuals (inter-individual consistency).

**Hypothesis (from `noise.md`):** If the CL tree topology is conserved despite noise, while FC edge sets diverge, this supports the proposal that CL's sparsity constraint filters out noise-driven connectivity. The noise simulation provides the simulation-side evidence for this hypothesis.

**Status:** Figures generated (`noise_sim_networks.pdf`, `noise_sim_consistency.pdf`); quantitative conclusions were documented in `misc/CL_notes.md` as "noise robustness inconclusive."

### 3.4 Coupling-Type Analysis

`mi_vs_correlation_demo.py` demonstrates the key nonlinearity advantage: for QUADRATURE (phase coupling), SQUARED, and PAC coupling types, |r| ≈ 0 but MI > 0. This is the theoretical motivation for using MI over FC.

**Important caveat (from `research.md`):** The main experimental design uses **linear coupling with heterogeneous per-node parameters**. The coupling-type nonlinearity advantage is real but not currently the primary focus; the current simulations inject variability through node parameter heterogeneity rather than nonlinear coupling. Future work should examine whether heterogeneous-parameter dynamics produce detectable nonlinear dependencies that MI captures and FC misses.

## 5. GCSS Motor Network Analysis

This is the most complete and detailed analysis in the repository. The GCSS approach (Fedorenko et al. 2010; Julian et al. 2012) derives individual-specific functional ROIs by intersecting each subject's motor task activation maps with group-level cluster boundaries.

### 5.1 Pipeline (Phases 1–5)

**Phase 1–2 (Group map construction):** Individual odd-run z-maps (avoiding the allruns inversion bug) are thresholded at z > 3.7 and overlaid to produce voxel-wise group activation probability maps. Watershed segmentation identifies connected clusters.

**Phase 3 (Subject-specific fROI extraction):** Each subject's z-map is intersected with group cluster boundaries. Six Tier-1 contrasts were used:

| Contrast | Group max prob | Cluster | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| LFoot | 0.90 | Right paracentral M1 | **Correct** |
| RFoot | 0.80 | Left paracentral M1 | **Correct** |
| LHand | 0.90 → 0.90* | Right M1 hand knob | **Correct after motor mask** |
| RHand | 0.90 → 0.90* | Left S1/M1 hand | **Correct after motor mask** |
| tongue | 1.00 | Bilateral lateral M1 + FOP2 | **Correct** |
| combined_motor | 0.90 | L_PMd, R_PMv, SMA, L/R_S1S2 | **Correct** |

*LHand and RHand had a VVC (visual cortex) artifact from task visual cues present in 9/10 subjects (max prob=0.90). Fixed by applying a motor cortex mask before watershed segmentation.

**Confirmed somatotopic gradient:**
```
Foot M1   z ≈ +69 mm (paracentral lobule)
Hand M1   z ≈ +58 mm (hand knob / omega-shaped gyrus)
Tongue M1 z ≈ +29 mm (lateral face M1)
```
This replicates the Penfield homunculus (Penfield & Rasmussen 1950) with modern precision in subject-specific data.

**Phase 4 (Resting-state timeseries extraction):** Resting-state BOLD was extracted through all fROI masks with the corrected confound model. All 100 sessions complete (8,180 TRs per subject, 10 sessions × 818 TRs).

**Phase 5 (Connectivity analysis):** FC, MI, and CL trees computed for the 11-ROI canonical scheme.

**Canonical CL tree edges (≥ 7/10 subjects):**

1. **L_M1_tongue ↔ R_M1_tongue** (10/10, MI=0.876): Universal bilateral coupling — anatomically grounded in obligate bilateral corticobulbar projections. The tongue is a midline organ; both M1 tongue areas project bilaterally to the hypoglossal nucleus. This is the most reliable edge in the dataset.

2. **L_M1_hand ↔ R_M1_hand** (10/10, MI=0.608): Interhemispheric hand M1 coupling via the corpus callosum. Mirror activity during unimanual movements is well-established (Swinnen 2002).

3. **L_M1_foot ↔ R_M1_foot** (10/10, MI=0.512): Bilateral foot M1 coupling; weaker than hand because lower-limb movements are more independent (walking requires alternating leg control, unlike bimanual coordination).

4. **L_M1_tongue ↔ L_S1S2** (9/10, MI=0.759): Left tongue M1 to left secondary somatosensory cortex (parietal operculum). Anatomically represents the orofacial sensorimotor feedback loop — proprioceptive and tactile signals from oral movements return via VPM-thalamus to S2 and back to M1.

5. **L_S1S2 ↔ R_S1S2** (8/10, MI=0.493): Bilateral SII coupling. Unlike S1, which is predominantly contralateral, S2 integrates input from both body sides (Hsiao et al. 2002), making bilateral S2 coupling neuroanatomically expected.

6. **R_M1_tongue ↔ R_PMv** (7/10, MI=0.556): Right tongue M1 to right ventral premotor cortex. PMv (area 6v in Glasser) coordinates orofacial motor sequences; this edge reflects orofacial motor planning circuitry.

Its role as a cross-effector sensorimotor integration node makes it a natural CL tree bridge.

This pair is consistently strong but never selected by the MST: whenever it could appear, the L_M1_tongue ↔ L_S1S2 and R_M1_tongue ↔ R_PMv edges already cover both nodes at higher MI, making this a "redundant" indirect path in the MST formulation. This illustrates an important property of CL trees: the MST selects direct dependencies and excludes indirect ones, even when the indirect path has high pairwise MI.

## 6. Key Findings and Interpretations

### 6.1 CL Trees Capture a Sparse, Conserved Motor Circuit Backbone

The three universal bilateral M1 pairs (tongue, hand, foot in that order) are present in all 10 MSC subjects. Their rank order — tongue > hand > foot — precisely mirrors the neuroanatomy:
- Tongue M1 is inherently bilateral (midline organ, obligate bilateral corticobulbar projections) → strongest interhemispheric coupling
- Hand M1 has extensive callosal connections and strong mirror activity → intermediate coupling
- Foot M1 has the weakest interhemispheric coupling (independent lower-limb control for walking) → weakest coupling

This ordering, emerging purely from MI and the CL MST, constitutes strong construct validity for the approach.

### 6.2 The CL Tree Detects Direct Dependencies Unmixed from Indirect Paths

The L_S1S2 ↔ R_M1_tongue pair has high group-mean MI (rank 4 overall) but zero CL frequency. In FC, this pair would appear as a high-correlation edge and would be difficult to interpret. The CL tree correctly identifies it as an indirect path mediated by L_M1_tongue. This demonstrates the fundamental advantage of the CL framework: it suppresses indirect "functional" connections that FC cannot distinguish from direct anatomical coupling.

This principle is relevant to clinical applications: FC edges in patient data (e.g., between a lesioned region and its downstream partners) may reflect indirect diaschisis effects; CL trees may more directly identify which connections are truly direct vs. propagated.

### 6.3 L_S1S2 as the Integration Hub — Beyond the Original Prediction

SMA was predicted to be the dominant hub. L_S1S2 (parietal operculum / SII) emerged instead. This is not a failure — it is a discovery. The result suggests that in resting-state data, the cross-effector sensorimotor convergence zone (SII) is more strongly positioned as the motor network's hub than the movement-initiation center (SMA). SII integrates proprioceptive, tactile, and efference-copy signals from all body parts, making it neuroanatomically ideal as a cross-effector bridge. This finding aligns with recent emphasis on SII's role in sensorimotor integration (Hsiao et al. 2002; Dijkerman & de Haan 2007) and its activation across all effectors in the MSC motor task.

### 6.4 Inter-Individual Variability — Simulation Hypothesis Partially Supported

The three bilateral M1 pairs are perfectly conserved (10/10 subjects). The premotor branch (SMA, PMd, PMv edges) is individually variable (4–7/10 subjects). This is consistent with the noise robustness hypothesis: the strongest MI edges (bilateral M1 pairs) are driven by robust anatomical connections and appear in every CL tree, while weaker edges (premotor planning) show individual variation potentially explained by noise, strategy differences, or genuine individual differences in motor planning circuits.

The individual-differences plan (`individual_difference_plan.md`) outlines a quantitative test: comparing CL edge Jaccard similarity across subjects vs. top-N FC edge Jaccard. The predicted direction (CL Jaccard > FC Jaccard) remains to be tested on this data.

### 6.5 Simulation Validates CL for Sparse Ground Truth

Under LINEAR coupling with heterogeneous node parameters, CL outperforms FC on sparse (tree-structured) ground truths across all tested N values and noise levels. The advantage diminishes for dense GT. This maps directly to the biological claim: if cortical circuits are sparse at the level of direct monosynaptic connections (as structural connectivity data suggests), CL trees are the appropriate statistical model for recovering them from functional data.

## 7. Current Limitations and Open Issues

### 7.1 ROI Size Confound

The `L_M1_tongue` ROI merges M1, FOP2, PoI2, and insular regions, making it much larger (1148 vox) than foot (184 vox) or hand (516 vox). MI is sensitive to ROI size through the effective spatial resolution of the signal. The tongue-dominated connectivity pattern may partially reflect ROI size rather than connection strength. A size-matched analysis (e.g., downsampling tongue ROI to 184 voxels) is needed.

### 7.2 Bilateral M1 Pairs Exhaust Tree Capacity

With 11 ROIs and 10 CL edges, the three bilateral M1 pairs (tongue, hand, foot) consume 6 edges — 60% of the tree — leaving only 4 edges for the entire premotor branch. This structural constraint limits the expressiveness of the 11-ROI CL tree for premotor organization. A partial MI or conditional-MI CL tree (conditioning on the bilateral pairs) would expose premotor connectivity more clearly.

### 7.3 Missing Test-Retest Reliability

Even-run fROIs (runs 02, 04, ..., 20) have not been extracted. The planned split-half validation (Fedorenko 2010 benchmark: within-subject r ≈ 0.52) would confirm fROI stability and provide a quantitative reliability estimate for the localizer approach.

### 7.4 Cerebellar and Subcortical GCSS Not Yet Implemented

GCSS Phases 3E (cerebellar SUIT-constrained fROIs) and 3F (putamen/thalamus fROIs) are planned but not yet implemented. These would extend the motor CL tree to include the full cortico-cerebellar-thalamo-striatal loop — the anatomical completeness needed to test the hierarchical motor circuit hypothesis (Buckner et al. 2011; Diedrichsen et al. 2019).

### 7.5 No Statistical Testing of CL vs. FC Differences

The individual-differences plan outlines paired t-tests comparing CL Jaccard vs. FC top-N Jaccard, but these tests have not been run. All results to date are descriptive. Statistical inference is needed before making strong claims about conservation differences.

### 7.6 Optimized Extraction Script Bug

`msc_extract_timeseries_optimized.py` has a syntax error on line 99 (missing comma in `NiftiLabelsMasker`). The parallel extraction path is non-functional.

### 7.7 No Test Suite

There is no automated test suite. Correctness is validated by reproducing figure types and cross-checking against TVB reference implementations.

## 8. Follow-Up Directions — Lesion Mapping and Perinatal Stroke

The most scientifically significant and differentiating extension of this work is **lesion-based validation**. The argument is as follows: if CL trees correctly recover direct anatomical connections, then lesions disrupting specific cortical or subcortical nodes should produce locally interpretable, anatomically predictable changes in CL tree topology. FC, being dense and conflating direct with indirect paths, would show distributed, hard-to-interpret disruption patterns that are less anatomically specific.

The **Laumann & Gordon perinatal stroke dataset** (Washington University, St. Louis) is ideal for this test. Perinatal stroke patients develop after a unilateral focal lesion at birth, typically in the territory of the middle cerebral artery (MCA). Because the brain undergoes substantial reorganization, these patients often show preserved function despite large structural lesions — but their functional connectivity is reorganized in characteristic ways. The precision imaging approach used by Laumann and Gordon (highly sampled individual fMRI: ~4–9 hours/subject) provides the data quality needed for reliable individual-level FC and CL tree estimation.

### 8.1 Why Perinatal Stroke is the Ideal Test Case

**Known lesion topology:** Unlike most focal stroke patients, perinatal stroke has predictable lesion location (MCA territory: lateral cortex, often including hand/foot M1, S1, premotor cortex on one side). The current GCSS motor parcellation defines precisely the ROIs most likely to be affected.

**Motor circuit relevance:** The 11-ROI GCSS scheme covers the bilateral motor network. A lesion destroying, say, left hemisphere hand M1 would be predicted to eliminate the L_M1_hand ↔ R_M1_hand edge from the CL tree (or drastically reduce its MI weight). FC would show complex, distributed changes across many pairs.

**Within-subject precision:** The Laumann/Gordon approach uses individual-specific parcellation (related to GCSS), making the analysis directly compatible with this pipeline.

**Developmental reorganization adds scientific value:** In perinatal stroke, surviving cortex near the lesion can take on functions of the destroyed tissue. CL trees, by identifying the backbone of direct dependencies, could reveal whether this reorganized connectivity follows the same canonical tree topology or creates novel structural patterns.

### 8.2 Specific Research Questions for Perinatal Stroke Data

**Question 1: Do CL trees predict behavioral impairment from lesion location more precisely than FC?**

Design: For each patient, compute the CL tree from resting-state fMRI. Identify which canonical GCSS edges are missing or weakened. Correlate edge loss with scores on motor tasks (grip strength, dexterity, foot-tapping speed) for the affected effector. Compare predictive power of CL-based edge disruption vs. FC-based connectivity.

Predicted result: CL edge disruption (binary: edge present/absent in canonical network) will be a stronger predictor of task-specific motor impairment than FC connectivity loss, because CL edges map to direct anatomical pathways while FC values conflate direct and indirect connections.

**Question 2: Is the CL tree topology more conserved across perinatal stroke patients than FC?**

Design: Compare CL Jaccard similarity across a cohort of perinatal stroke patients (between patients with similar lesion location) to Jaccard similarity in healthy controls. If the motor circuit backbone is preserved despite lesion (due to reorganization), CL trees may show higher inter-patient similarity than FC.

This directly tests the Milo e-mail hypothesis in a clinical context: that CL trees recover a conserved motor circuit template even in pathological brains, while FC captures lesion-induced noise.

**Question 3: Do CL trees reveal reorganization patterns that FC misses?**

Design: Compare CL trees in patients with equivalent lesion volumes but different functional outcomes (good vs. poor recovery). The CL tree topology (which edges are present, which nodes serve as hubs) may differentiate recovery trajectories better than FC strength.

Predicted finding (based on stroke literature): In patients with good recovery, ipsilesional premotor cortex (PMd, SMA) may take over hub roles previously held by the damaged M1. In the CL tree, this would appear as a shift in hub node identity from M1 to PMd/SMA — directly observable as a topological change.

**Question 4: Does CL tree topology correlate with structural connectivity (DTI tractography)?**

Design: If tractography data exists for the same patients, compare CL tree edge presence to structural tract presence (e.g., corticospinal tract integrity measured by DTI FA along the tract). For patients with partial corticospinal tract loss, the MI on the corresponding CL edge should decrease proportionally to tract integrity.

This would provide the strongest validation: CL tree edges track direct structural connections, and when the structural connection is lost, the CL edge disappears.

### 8.3 Implementation Plan for Lesion Mapping Analysis

**Step 1 — Patient GCSS fROI extraction:**
Run the GCSS pipeline (Phase 1–5 of `gcss_plan.md`) on perinatal stroke patients. For affected hemispheres, expect reduced activation in M1/S1 parcels within the lesion territory. The Phase 3 intersection step will naturally produce smaller or absent fROIs for lesioned parcels — this absence itself is informative (comparable to the "minimum voxel fallback" handling already implemented).

A lesion mask should be applied to exclude lesioned voxels from the fROI definition. This requires:
- Binary lesion mask per patient (typically from manual segmentation on T1w)
- Pre-masking the atlas volume with the lesion exclusion mask before fROI extraction

**Step 2 — Edge disruption quantification:**
For each patient, compute MI(e) for each of the 6 canonical CL edges, normalized by the healthy-control group mean MI(e). Report:
- `disruption[e]` = 1 − MI_patient(e) / MI_control_mean(e)
- Which canonical edges are missing (CL frequency in patients vs. 10/10 in controls)

**Step 3 — Behavioral correlation:**
Correlate `disruption[hand_bilateral]` with motor outcome (grip strength asymmetry, Fugl-Meyer, or Box-and-Block score). Repeat for foot and tongue edges. **Step 4 — CL vs. FC comparison of predictive power:**
For the same patients, compute FC-based disruption metrics (how much has the mean FC of the canonical hand/foot/tongue pairs dropped?). Cross-validate the CL and FC predictive models using leave-one-patient-out (appropriate for the small N typical in precision imaging cohorts).

### 8.4 Broader Lesion Mapping Vision

The framework generalizes beyond motor cortex. Using the full Glasser360+SUIT+Thalamus combined atlas:

**Whole-brain CL tree disruption mapping:** For each lesioned patient, compare their full-brain CL tree to the healthy-subject consensus tree. Edges that are consistently disrupted across patients with similar lesion topographies identify the most vulnerable edges in the motor and sensorimotor networks. This is analogous to "lesion network mapping" (Fox et al. 2014; Boes et al. 2015), where stroke symptom location is mapped back to brain networks, but here applied to tree-structured connectivity.

**Comparison with lesion network mapping:** Fox et al. (2014) showed that neurological symptoms from focal lesions map onto specific brain networks defined by resting-state FC. Replacing FC with CL tree edges in this framework would produce a sparser, more anatomically interpretable lesion network map — each symptom dimension would map onto specific CL tree edges rather than diffuse FC networks.

**Thalamo-cortical disconnection:** The Morel thalamus atlas already in the pipeline (VLa, VLpd/VLpv motor relay nuclei) could be used to test whether thalamic damage disrupts the cortico-thalamic edges in CL trees. Many MCA strokes involve posterior limb of the internal capsule (thalamocortical fibers), and CL trees might detect this disconnection as disruption of specific thalamo-cortical MI edges.

**Cerebellar stroke and diaschisis:** The SUIT atlas enables cerebellar CL tree analysis. Cerebellar stroke, or cortical stroke causing crossed cerebellar diaschisis (contralateral cerebellar hypometabolism after cortical injury), could be tracked through CL tree edge changes. The cortico-cerebellar edges (motor cortex → lobule V/VI) predicted by Buckner et al. (2011) should weaken when the motor cortex is lesioned.
